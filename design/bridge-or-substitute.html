Bridge or Substitute

A three-study program at Universiti Malaya, Department of Educational Psychology and Counseling.

Does talking to an AI companion lead Malaysian men toward help from friends, family and counselors, or away from it? We think self-compassion is the key.

Background and rationale

Many men wait until a crisis. AI could change that, in either direction.

AI chatbots now give Malaysian men something they have rarely had: a private, judgment-free place to talk at any hour. Nobody yet knows whether that leads them toward people or away from them.

Men, masculine norms and face

Malaysian men are less likely than women to seek help for psychological distress, and many wait until they reach a crisis. Conformity to masculine norms, particularly self-reliance, is consistently linked to poorer mental health and more negative attitudes toward seeking help (Addis & Mahalik, 2003; Wong et al., 2017).

In Malaysia these norms are reinforced by concern for face. For many men, admitting they are struggling feels like failing.

The evidence points both ways

Bridge

Chatbots reduce distress and build self-compassion

Conversational agents reduce depressive symptoms, and brief chatbot conversations have increased self-compassion.

Li et al., 2023 · Lee et al., 2019

Substitute

Heavier use, more loneliness

In a four-week randomized study by MIT Media Lab and OpenAI, heavier chatbot use was linked to loneliness and less contact with other people.

Fang et al., 2025

The proposed mechanism: self-compassion

Self-compassion is the capacity to treat yourself with kindness during difficulty (Neff, 2003). By buffering the shame that masculine norms attach to asking for help, it may make men more willing to reach out. Heath et al. (2017) found exactly this among men: the link between masculine norms and self-stigma was weaker for those higher in self-compassion.

The program draws on the Theory of Planned Behavior (Ajzen, 1991) and tests whether an AI companion increases actual help-seeking from people, and why.

H1 is tested in Study 2; H2 to H4 are estimated in Study 2 and tested in Study 3.

Why the answer matters either way

If Sol is a bridge

Universities and community services gain a low-cost, culturally fitted way to reach men who would never walk into a counseling room.

If Sol is a substitute

Developers and regulators gain clear evidence that AI tools must be designed to hand users over to human support.

This builds on our earlier survey of 210 Malaysian undergraduates, where social connection (r = .43) and empathy (r = .53) were linked to willingness to seek help (Chong, Ng, Ban et al., 2022).

Research design

Three studies, one question

Each study answers one question and feeds the next. Study 3 goes ahead only if Study 2 meets its progression criteria.

Ora and Sol

Ora is the study app. Sol is the AI companion inside it. Both already exist as a working prototype, with a safety escalation protocol and a researcher console, so the trials can begin as soon as ethics approval is granted. Every safety flag is followed up by a person within 24 hours.

Study 1 · Scoping review

What is already known?

We are mapping published research on AI and mental health help-seeking in Asia, with particular attention to men. The gaps we find will shape the design of Sol and the outcomes we measure in Studies 2 and 3.

DesignScoping review following JBI and PRISMA-ScR
WhenOctober 2026 to January 2027
StatusUnderway
Study 2 · Feasibility trial

Can a trial work with Malaysian men?

A small randomized trial of Ora and Sol with young Malaysian men. It tests whether a full trial is workable and safe, and gives a first look at whether Sol works as a bridge or a substitute. Findings will be written up as manuscripts for publication.

DesignFeasibility randomized controlled trial with interviews
WhoMen aged 18 to 35 in Malaysia
WhenRecruitment from January 2027, after ethics approval
Study 3 · Full trial

Does Sol work, and why?

A larger trial across several Malaysian settings to give a clear answer on whether Sol helps men reach people, and whether self-compassion explains the effect. It goes ahead only if Study 2 shows a full trial is workable.

DesignMulti-site randomized controlled trial
WhereMalaysian universities and community settings
WhenFrom 2028, if Study 2 says go

Before you apply, ask yourself what you want from the experience. For some people it is a step toward a Master's or PhD; for others it is a way to find out whether research, or building technology for mental health, suits them at all. Research team

Meet the people behind Bridge or Substitute, a three-study program running from 2026 to 2029 in the Department of Educational Psychology and Counseling, Universiti Malaya.

BB

Brendon Ban Ren Jie

PI

Master of Professional Counseling, Universiti Malaya

Brendon is a counseling trainee who understands both the clinical side and the technology. He designed and built Ora and Sol, including the safety escalation protocol and the researcher console, so the trials can start as soon as ethics approval is granted. He leads all three studies: he runs the Study 1 search and screening, and he is writing up each study as a manuscript for publication.

His earlier work, a survey of 210 Malaysian undergraduates presented at MAPSA 2022, found that social connection and empathy were linked to willingness to seek help. The program builds on that finding.
